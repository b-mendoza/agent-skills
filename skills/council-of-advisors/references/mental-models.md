# Mental Models Used by the Council

Read this file when a seat needs to name its mental model in the
`mental_model_in_use` field, or when authoring the educate-me lesson card
for a seat. Each model below is one paragraph — enough to ground the
seat's reasoning, not a substitute for studying the source.

## Reversibility and Type 1 / Type 2 decisions

Some decisions are doors that swing both ways: try the choice, observe
what happens, walk back if it was wrong. Other decisions are doors that
close behind you: the cost of reversal is so high — in money, time,
identity, reputation, relationships, optionality — that for practical
purposes the decision is permanent. Most decisions are reversible (Type 2)
and reward speed; the few that are irreversible (Type 1) reward
deliberation. The classification error people make most often is treating
Type 1 decisions like Type 2 — casually committing to moves that are
expensive or impossible to undo.

## Inversion (used by the adversary seat)

Instead of asking "how do I succeed?", ask "what would guarantee
failure?" — then refuse to do those things. Inversion catches risks that
forward-only thinking smooths over, because the human tendency is to
imagine the success path and downplay the failure path. The adversary
seat uses inversion to steel-man the case against the user's plan,
citing specific failure modes and precedents rather than arguing from
preference.

## Asymmetric bets (used by the optimistic seat)

Look for situations where the downside is bounded and small while the
upside is large or unbounded. A bet where you can lose a small known
amount but win a large or open-ended amount is asymmetric in your
favor — and a series of such bets compounds. The optimistic seat names
the conditions under which the upside materializes so the asymmetry can
be checked rather than assumed.

## Prior art and differentiation (used by the originality seat)

Reinventing a solved problem produces work but not compounding return
unless the new version is differentiated in a way the market actually
rewards: better, cheaper, faster, narrower, broader, more accessible, or
more trusted. The originality seat asks whether incumbents exist and
where the user's solution measurably differs — not whether the user
believes it differs.

## Second-order thinking

First-order effects are the immediate consequences of a choice. Second-
and third-order effects are what happens next, and next after that, as
other people respond and conditions evolve. Most plans fail not on the
first-order outcome but on the chain of consequences that follow.
Second-order thinking asks "and then what?" until the chain breaks or
loops, and labels each branch with likelihood and time-to-materialize.

## The paradox of skill

As average skill rises across a competitive field, the relative
advantage of any one participant's skill shrinks, and luck becomes the
dominant variable in outcomes. In highly saturated fields, individual
skill matters less than timing, distribution, or capital. The
paradox-of-skill seat estimates how much variance is luck in the user's
specific domain so the user can size their expectations honestly rather
than crediting future success to skill alone.

## Concentration and opportunity cost

Concentrated effort compounds; dispersed effort does not. The true cost
of pursuing a thing is not the money it costs — it is the next-best
thing the same attention would have produced. The focus seat asks what
the user would have to stop doing in order to give this the
concentrated attention it requires, and whether the displacement is
acceptable.

## Power questions

The quality of a decision is bounded by the quality of the questions
that shaped it. The most leverage in a decision usually comes from
asking the question the decider is most uncomfortable with — identity
questions, falsifiability questions, cost-of-inaction questions, and
counter-position questions. The power-questions seat surfaces the
highest-leverage questions the user is not asking, ranked by what their
answer would change.

## Independent synthesis (used by the chair seat)

A council adds value only if its members reason independently. The
chair's job is to integrate, not vote — to name agreements, characterize
disagreements (factual, interpretive, values-based, confidence-based),
weight by evidence quality rather than opinion strength, and preserve
the strongest dissent verbatim when confidence is below high. A chair
that fabricates consensus erases the council's reason to exist.
